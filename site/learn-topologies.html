The core topologies

Every classic DC-DC converter is the same three ingredients: a switch, a diode or second switch, and an inductor. They are arranged so that volt-second balance forces a particular ratio. Why the lossy ratio collapses

With resistance, the boost and buck-boost ratios peak and then fall as D approaches 1. The inductor current is Iout/(1 - D), so its I^2R loss grows as 1/(1 - D)^2 while the ideal gain grows only as 1/(1 - D). Past the peak, more duty cycle means less output. That is the real reason single-stage boost converters stop at roughly 4-6x, and why high step-up designs turn to switched capacitors (lesson 3).

boost:  M = 1/(1-D) * 1 / (1 + (R_on + R_L) / ((1-D)^2 * R))

The four-switch buck-boost, and where the flagship starts

The non-inverting four-switch buck-boost joins a buck half-bridge to a boost half-bridge through one inductor. In buck mode the output leg just stays on, and in boost mode the input leg does. Only near Vin = Vout do both legs switch, and that costs efficiency (the flagship's efficiency curve dips there). The flagship keeps this skeleton and replaces each half-bridge with a three-level flying-capacitor leg; lesson 3 explains why.
