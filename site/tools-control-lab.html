Control lab

A converter is only as efficient as the controller running it. This lab races eight maximum power point tracking (MPPT) algorithms against the same simulated panel, sensors and test profiles. It then turns to the controls that cut the converter's own losses: frequency, dead time, burst mode and bypass. Every number here is computed in your browser, by the same code the repository tests in Python and C.

A day of sun

One clear day, 06:00 to 18:00, with a cloudy hour after noon. The panel feeds a 51.2 V battery through the flagship converter. This is where the energy goes, with each control switched on in turn.

Method

Plant. The panel is a 72-cell single-diode model with three bypass-diode substrings, simulated every 20 ms. The converter's input-voltage loop closes 98 % of the error to the commanded voltage each tick. Sensors add noise (sigma 20 mV and 10 mA) and 12-bit quantisation over 0-72 V and 0-20 A. A tracker sees the settled half of each update window.

Profiles. The two ramp profiles keep the EN 50530 dynamic-test shape: trapezoids between 30 % and 100 % of nominal irradiance, and between 10 % and 50 %. They use four of the standard slopes each, with 10 s dwells. The standard itself repeats slopes with longer dwells, so this is a compressed test, not a compliance test. The cloud and partial-shading profiles test fast transients and multiple power maxima. The steady profile includes each tracker's start-up search.

Fairness. Every tracker gets the same kind of tuning: a grid of 8 to 12 combinations over its two most important parameters, on a separate tuning profile (scripts/tune_mppt.py). Grids were re-centred until each winner was bracketed by lower-scoring values or sat at a physical floor (P&O updating every tick, an 8-point scan). Particle swarm's restart threshold was bracketed across two passes: 0.25 beat 0.15 in one and 0.35 in the other. The tuning profile includes shade, which pulls fractional Voc's k away from its best value in even light. The lesson discusses this. Results are the mean of three noise seeds.

Efficiency controls. The controls are evaluated on the flagship loss model, including its dead-time physics: incomplete soft switching when the dead time is too short, reverse conduction when it is too long. The frequency and dead-time hill climbs see the loss only through noisy power measurements: the bench's sensors, averaged over 5 s (about 25-35 mW resolution). Each loss shown is the mean over 20 noise trials. Burst-mode and bypass settings are design-time choices made with the model. Burst mode pays for its input-capacitor ripple: the panel wanders off its maximum power point between bursts, so the loss counts kappa x dV2/24, where kappa is the curvature of the power-voltage curve. Ripple is capped at 5 % of the panel voltage, and kappa is scaled from the reference panel as P/V2. Bypass pays (1/2) kappa (V_mpp - V_bat)2 for pinning the panel. Housekeeping power and the wake-up energy per burst are estimates.

Same code everywhere. The trackers and the hill climb exist in Python (watt_forge/control), JavaScript (this page) and C (wf_mppt.c). The tests require JavaScript to match Python to rounding error, and C to match Python decision for decision. All three are reference implementations: simulate and review before use.
