Fundamentals

A switching converter moves energy in packets. A switch chops the input, an inductor or capacitor holds the energy for a fraction of a microsecond, and the average of the chopped waveform becomes the output. Nothing in the ideal circuit dissipates power, which is why converters can reach 99 %.

Duty cycle

Intuition. Flip a switch on for part of each period and off for the rest. The fraction of time it is on is the duty cycle, D. If the switch connects a node to Vin while on and to ground while off, the node's average voltage is D x Vin. A buck converter is exactly that, followed by a low-pass filter (an inductor and a capacitor) that keeps the average and throws away the chopping.

D = t_on / Ts = t_on * fs    <v_sw> = D * Vin
t_on
switch on-time [s]
Ts, fs
switching period [s] and frequency [Hz], Ts = 1/fs
<v_sw>
period-average of the switch-node voltage [V]
Worked example. 48 V to 12 V needs D = 12/48 = 0.25: at 200 kHz (Ts = 5 us) the switch is on for 1.25 us of every 5 us. Checked in tests/test_ratios.py::test_ideal_ratios_worked_examples

Volt-second balance

Intuition. An inductor's current changes at a rate v/L. If the current is to come back to where it started every period (steady state), the voltage across the inductor must average to zero: the positive "volt-seconds" while the switch is on must equal the negative volt-seconds while it is off. That single rule gives every conversion ratio in lesson 2. Drag D below: when the two shaded areas are equal, the inductor current closes on itself.

<v_L> = (1/Ts) * integral(v_L dt) = 0   =>   D*(Vin - Vout) - (1 - D)*Vout = 0   =>   Vout = D*Vin (buck)

Charge balance

Intuition. The dual rule for capacitors: a capacitor's voltage changes at i/C, so in steady state its current must average to zero. Charge delivered while the switch is off must equal charge drawn while it is on. For a boost converter's output capacitor, the load draws Iout all the time, and the inductor refills it only during (1 - D). So the inductor current must be Iout/(1 - D), which is why boost converters carry large inductor currents at high step-up.

<i_C> = 0   =>   -Iout*D + (IL - Iout)*(1 - D) = 0   =>   IL = Iout / (1 - D)
Worked example. A boost at D = 0.5 delivering 2 A needs 4 A of inductor current. tests/test_ratios.py::test_volt_second_and_charge_balance

Continuous vs discontinuous conduction (CCM / DCM)

Intuition. The inductor current is a triangle riding on its average. At light load the triangle's valley can reach zero. With a diode as the second switch, the current then stops for part of the period: discontinuous conduction mode (DCM). The conversion ratio then depends on the load, not just on D. A synchronous converter (a second transistor instead of a diode) lets the current go negative instead. It stays in "forced CCM", which costs some circulating current but, as lesson 4 shows, can buy soft switching.

K = 2L / (R*Ts)   CCM when K > K_crit(D):  buck 1 - D,  boost D(1 - D)^2,  buck-boost (1 - D)^2
